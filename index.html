About

I am a PhD candidate in Economics at the Norwegian School of Economics (NHH), where I expect to complete my degree in August 2026. In Spring 2026 I am a visiting scholar at Cornell University, hosted by Heather Schofield.

My research sits at the intersection of development, environmental, and health economics. I study how environmental change and economic conditions shape health and livelihoods in developing countries — from the forest and health co-benefits of solar power expansion in rural China, to the way heat and economic stress interact to affect mental health in Indonesia, and how mental health shapes labor market participation in rural Ghana.

Before my PhD, I earned an MSc in Economics from NHH and an MA in Economics of Education from Central China Normal University, with an exchange semester at National Taiwan University of Science and Technology.

Research

Job Market Paper

When Solar Reaches the Poor: Forest and Health Co-Benefits in Rural China working paper

Single-authored

Abstract

Solar power is expanding into poor rural areas worldwide, yet little is known about what grid-connected solar does to the communities that host it beyond producing electricity. I study China's Solar Energy Poverty Alleviation Program, which built solar stations in designated poverty counties and assigned registered poor households as beneficiaries. Combining the program registry with satellite forest-loss data and a national household panel, I find that forest loss around host villages fell by roughly 13 percent during the program era, and that the probability a child's most serious recorded illness is respiratory fell by 11 percentage points, about 18 percent of the sample mean. Placebo illness categories and poverty counties without the program show no detected change. Satellite-detected solar plants outside the program show no comparable forest decline, suggesting that the poverty-campaign link, not the technology alone, drives the response. The local consequences of solar investment depend on where and how it is deployed.

Publications

Psychological Barriers to Participation in the Labor Market: Evidence from Rural Ghana published

with Leandro Carvalho, Damien de Walque, Crick Lund, Heather Schofield, Vincent Somville

Journal of Development Economics, 2026, 103734

Abstract

Mental health conditions are strongly associated with reduced labor market participation, but the underlying channels through which such conditions impact labor supply remain unclear. We conduct a two-phase study decomposing this relationship by examining (i) job take-up decisions, (ii) labor supply, output, and earnings conditional on job take-up, and (iii) quit rates. In Phase 1, women in rural Ghana are asked whether they would be willing to take up a cash-for-work job during the lean season when alternative work is scarce. We find that individuals with depression and anxiety, which are common in this population, are much more likely to decline work offers outside the home but equally likely to accept work-from-home positions. In Phase 2, we randomly offer jobs at home to those who were willing to work from home, avoiding selection effects. Neither depression nor anxiety predict work completion, income, or quit rates. These findings suggest that poor mental health may harm labor market outcomes in traditional jobs outside of the home via reduced take-up, above and beyond the established negative impacts of mental health on productivity in work outside of the home. But the results also suggest an alternative approach to improving labor market outcomes for those in poor mental health: work-from-home opportunities, which are not associated with lower take-up or lower productivity on the job for those in poor mental health.

Working Papers

Heat, Economic Stress, and Mental Health: Evidence from Indonesia working paper

with Simon Taye, Damien de Walque, Heather Schofield, Hao Wang

Abstract

The potential for heat to harm mental health is well-known, but inconsistent across populations. We explore a potential source of heterogeneity in this effect that may drive these conflicting results: underlying economic stress. We draw on panel data from Indonesia and capture three types of economic shocks: income losses driven by international price changes for palm oil, sudden and large fuel cost increases, and job loss. In the general population we find no association between temperature and depressive symptoms — captured via the CES-D scale — and are able to rule out even small harmful effects. But for those experiencing a negative economic shock, we find robust negative impacts of heat ranging from 0.044 to 0.09 SD per degree Celsius. As climate change increases both the frequency of extreme heat and the prevalence of economic shocks, our findings suggest the psychological toll of a warming climate will be concentrated among the most economically vulnerable.
