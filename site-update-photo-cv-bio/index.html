About

I am a PhD candidate in Economics at the Norwegian School of Economics (NHH), supervised by Vincent Somville and Nicole Wägner. In Spring 2026, I was a visiting scholar at Cornell University, hosted by Heather Schofield.

My research fields are development, environmental, and health economics. I study the causes and consequences of physical and mental health in developing contexts, and how they relate to renewable energy expansion, climate change, and labor market decisions. My work combines satellite data, survey data, and field experiments.

Before my PhD, I earned an MSc in Economics from NHH and an MA in Economics of Education from Central China Normal University.

I am on the 2026–2027 academic job market.

Research

Job Market Paper

When Solar Reaches the Poor: Forest and Health Co-Benefits in Rural China working paper

Single-authored

Abstract

Solar power is expanding into poor rural areas worldwide, yet little is known about what grid-connected solar does to the communities that host it beyond producing electricity. I study China's Solar Energy Poverty Alleviation Program, which built solar stations in and around designated poverty counties and assigned registered poor households as beneficiaries. Combining the program registry with satellite forest-loss data and a national household panel, I find that forest loss within 15 kilometers of host villages fell by roughly 12 percent during the program era relative to cells farther away, and that the probability that a child's most serious recorded illness is respiratory fell by 11 percentage points, about 18 percent of the sample mean. Placebo illness categories and poverty counties without the program show no detected change. Around other detected solar plants, not matched to program villages, forest loss rose instead: the decline is specific to the program's host villages, not to solar deployment as such. The local consequences of solar investment depend on where and how it is deployed.

Publications

Psychological Barriers to Participation in the Labor Market: Evidence from Rural Ghana published

with Leandro Carvalho, Damien de Walque, Crick Lund, Heather Schofield, Vincent Somville

Journal of Development Economics, 181 (2026), 103734

Abstract

Mental health conditions are strongly associated with lower labor market participation, but the underlying channels remain unclear. We conduct a two-phase study of women in rural Ghana to examine job take-up and work outcomes. In the first phase, we ask whether women would accept cash-for-work jobs during the lean season. Women with depression or anxiety are much more likely to decline work outside the home, but equally likely to accept work-from-home positions. In the second phase, we randomly offer home-based jobs to women willing to work from home. Neither depression nor anxiety predicts work completion, earnings, or quit rates. These findings suggest that barriers to accepting work outside the home help explain the association between mental health and labor market participation, and highlight home-based employment as a potential response.

Working Papers

Heat, Economic Stress, and Mental Health: Evidence from Indonesia working paper

with Simon Taye, Damien de Walque, Heather Schofield, Hao Wang

Abstract

Evidence on the relationship between heat and mental health varies across populations. We examine whether underlying economic stress explains this heterogeneity using panel data from Indonesia and three types of economic shocks: income losses driven by international palm oil price changes, sudden large increases in fuel costs, and job loss. In the general population, we find no association between temperature and depressive symptoms, measured using the CES-D scale, and can rule out even small harmful effects. Among those experiencing negative economic shocks, however, heat is associated with increases in depressive symptoms of 0.044–0.09 standard deviations per degree Celsius. As climate change increases both the frequency of extreme heat and the prevalence of economic shocks, our findings suggest that the psychological toll of a warming climate will be concentrated among the most economically vulnerable.
